Astronomy / THE NOTEBOOK

The geometry behind the sky

Hasan Alseba · Adapted from my September 2026 handout · 2 min read

Spherical astronomy connects a familiar mathematical question—how do we describe a point?—to the sky above us.

I wrote my spherical astronomy handout to help fellow students connect the mathematical foundations to astronomical coordinates. This is a short introduction to its structure.

Begin with a sphere

A plane through the center of a sphere cuts out a great circle. Three suitable great-circle arcs can bound a spherical triangle. Its sides are arcs, so we describe them using the angles they subtend at the sphere’s center.

If the sphere has radius r and an arc subtends an angle c measured in radians, its length is rc. Keeping the angular measure separate from the physical length makes the geometry easier to follow.

Describe a point with angles

On a unit sphere, let θ measure elevation above the xy plane, and let ψ measure the angle in that plane from the positive x axis. Projecting the point onto the axes gives:

x = cos θ cos ψ
y = cos θ sin ψ
z = sin θ

The geometry comes from two right triangles: one separates the vertical component from the projection onto the xy plane; the other separates that projection into x and y components.

Change the coordinates, keep the point

The handout next rotates the coordinate axes about x. In the convention used there, a rotation of the axes by χ gives:

x′ = x
y′ = y cos χ + z sin χ
z′ = z cos χ − y sin χ

Substituting the angular components into these expressions links the original angles to the new ones. From that foundation, the handout derives spherical-triangle relations and introduces horizontal and equatorial coordinate systems.

Connect the mathematics to observation

The horizontal system uses the observer’s horizon, with altitude measuring height above it. The equatorial system uses the celestial equator, with declination measuring angular distance from it. The observer’s latitude determines how these reference planes are oriented relative to each other.

The interactive diagram on the homepage illustrates one consequence: for a northern observer in the idealized geometry, the north celestial pole’s altitude equals the observer’s latitude.
