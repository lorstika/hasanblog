From symmetry to conservation

Hasan Alseba · Updated 13 November 2026 · 2 min read

What surprises me about symmetry is its connection to conservation laws. I want to understand why a symmetry in the mathematics tells us that momentum or energy is conserved.

Symmetry comes up in many subjects, but this connection in physics especially interests me. Noether’s theorem relates continuous symmetries of the action to conservation laws. In mechanics, spatial translations are associated with momentum, and time translations with energy. The examples below show how this works for a particle moving in one dimension.

When position does not appear in L

Consider a particle of constant mass m moving along one dimension, with position x and velocity v = dx/dt. For the examples here, its Lagrangian is L = T − V. The Euler–Lagrange equation reads:

d/dt (∂L/∂v) = ∂L/∂x

If L does not depend explicitly on x, the right-hand side is zero. The quantity p = ∂L/∂v stays constant. For a free particle, L = mv²/2, so p = mv: the familiar linear momentum. Translating a trajectory by a constant distance leaves this Lagrangian unchanged.

Time and energy

When L has no explicit time dependence, the conserved quantity is:

E = v(∂L/∂v) − L

Along a trajectory satisfying the Euler–Lagrange equation, differentiating this expression gives dE/dt = −∂L/∂t. For a free particle, E = mv²/2.

Comparing a free particle with a spring

Now attach the particle to an ideal spring with stiffness k and an equilibrium position x = 0:

L = mv²/2 − kx²/2

This Lagrangian depends on x, so the particle’s momentum is not conserved. It still has no explicit time dependence, and its energy E = mv²/2 + kx²/2 is conserved. Here the spring is fixed and friction is ignored.

The spring gives a useful comparison: the particle’s momentum changes, but its total energy stays constant. We have to check each symmetry separately.

Study note on standard classical mechanics. Further reading: MIT OpenCourseWare on conservation of momentum and Noether’s theorem, and Symmetries and Conservation Laws.
